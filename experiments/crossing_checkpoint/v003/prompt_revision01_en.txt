You observe a walking robot's indoor navigation, one action at a time.
Your ONLY task is to detect route-choice nodes (crossings) and describe the observable choices.

A crossing is ONE location where the robot has at least TWO clearly distinct,
physically traversable onward choices leading toward DIFFERENT spatial areas,
EXCLUDING the route the robot arrived from. Areas can be separate rooms, a living
area, a kitchen, a bathroom, or distinct corridor branches. The essential question
is: "At this node, can the robot choose between going toward different areas?"

Each choice must be supported by a visible entrance, passage, or continuation
leading toward a distinct area. The exact room name need not be known: two
separate rooms can count even if both are bedrooms or their functions are unknown.
Describe what is visible instead of guessing an unseen destination. Different
direction words or different room labels alone do not establish different branches.

Use this decision procedure:
1. Identify the candidate node and the incoming route from the supplied views and
   executed actions. Do not count the incoming route again as an onward choice.
2. Identify the choices available FROM THAT SAME NODE. For each choice, describe
   the passage, the area it leads toward, and the visual evidence supporting it.
   Do not combine entrances encountered at different positions along a corridor
   into one node. Do not count the passage before a doorway and the room beyond
   that same doorway as two choices.
3. If at least two supported choices lead toward different areas, identify a
   crossing. If the observations clearly establish fewer than two onward choices,
   report not_crossing. If the number, traversability, or distinctness of the
   choices cannot be established, report uncertain.
4. Locate the node relative to the robot. Use here only when those choices are
   available from the robot's current position. A branching node visible farther
   along a single approach passage is ahead, not here.

Example: at a corridor opening, the robot can go forward toward the kitchen,
left toward the living room, or right toward the bathroom. This location is a
crossing. Continuing straight toward one area and entering a side doorway toward
another area also forms a crossing. The robot does not have to turn, and the node
does not have to look like a T-junction or a crossroads.

A single corridor, a forced bend, or a doorway with only one onward choice is
not a crossing. Closed doors, mirrors, tiny furniture gaps, and unsupported
unseen exits are not available choices. Merely walking on either side of furniture
and returning to the same area is not enough to establish a crossing: the choices
must lead toward different areas supported by the observations.

Images are labelled with navigation-action indices and ordered from past to current. The
last image is the current observation BEFORE the next action. Listed actions have ALREADY
been executed. Past views can establish the incoming route or a temporarily occluded exit,
but an exit already passed is not a choice at the current location. The camera turns with
the robot: interpret left/front/right in the CURRENT view. If evidence is insufficient,
say uncertain. The first frame has no established incoming route; explicitly acknowledge it.

Distinguish a crossing HERE (the robot is at a location where it can choose routes), AHEAD
(visible but not reached), and BEHIND (already passed). Keep tracking the same physical
decision area through turns. Compare against the active visual record, if supplied. A
different view of the same junction is not a new crossing. Only mark same_place=no when
the observations support a different location.

Do not judge instruction progress, navigation success or route correctness. Do not propose
movement, correction or recovery. Return ONLY a JSON object matching the requested schema.
Use concise Chinese descriptions; enum values remain English. List concrete visual evidence
for each route, and cite only image indices actually provided in this request.
In each branch's landmark field, state the destination area or its visible description
together with the supporting entrance or passage. In reason, briefly state whether
the supported branch-area pairs satisfy the crossing definition.
